Key Vocabulary 📚

  • Anthropometrics 📏 – Measurement of human body dimensions (from Greek: anthro = people, metrics = measure)
  • Ergonomics 🪑 – Designing for human comfort, safety, and efficiency
  • Percentile 🔢 – Statistical value showing how a measurement compares to a population
  • Biomechanics 🏋️ – Study of movement and forces on the human body
  • Physiological factors 💪 – Physical characteristics affecting performance (e.g. strength, stamina)
  • Psychological factors 🧠 – Mental processes affecting interaction (e.g. perception, memory)
  • Static data 🧍 – Measurements when the body is still
  • Dynamic data 🏃 – Measurements when the body is moving
  • Inclusive design ♿ – Designing products accessible to as many people as possible
  • Usability 👍 – How easy and satisfying a product is to use

📊 Data Concepts

📏 Measurement & Data Collection

How do we measure and collect information about people for design?

Anthropometrics 📏

Anthropometrics = people measurement. Designers use this science to make sure products fit real users.
Anthro = people, metrics = measure.

Example: Door handles are placed at a height most people can reach, based on anthropometric data. 🚪

Static & Structural Data 🧍

Static data: measuring the body while still (e.g., height, arm length). Structural data: measurements from a fixed position.

Example: Measuring height with a height gauge. 📏

Dynamic & Functional Data 🏃

Dynamic data: measuring the body in motion (e.g., reach, reaction time). Functional data: collected while doing a task (e.g., reaching for something).

Example: Measuring how far you can reach while sitting at a desk. 🖐️

Primary vs. Secondary Data 📊

Primary data: you collect it yourself (e.g., measuring classmates).
Secondary data: someone else collected it (e.g., US Army database).

🔢 Understanding & Using Data

How do we interpret, categorize, and use data to make better design decisions?

Percentiles 🔢

Designers use percentiles (like 5th, 50th, 95th) to fit the majority.
For example, a car seat might adjust from the 5th percentile female to the 95th percentile male. 🚗

Example: If you only design shoes for the 95th percentile, you'll go out of business fast! 👟

Types of Data 🔠

  • Interval (numeric, exact differences, e.g., temperature)
  • Nominal (names/categories, e.g., car types)
  • Ordinal (ranked, e.g., chili pepper hotness)
  • Qualitative (descriptive, e.g., how people feel)
  • Quantitative (measured with numbers, e.g., height)

Range of Sizes & Adjustability 📏↔️

Designers often create products with a range of sizes (like shoes) or adjustability (like adjustable skates or desks) to fit more people.

Example: Adjustable inline skates grow with your feet! 🛼

🪑 Design Concepts

What are Human Factors & Ergonomics? 🧑‍🔬

It's about making products, systems, and environments fit the people who use them. ✔️ The goal: comfort, safety, and effectiveness for everyone.

Example: A chair with adjustable height and lumbar support fits more users and reduces back pain. 🪑

Clearance, Reach & Workspace Envelope 🖐️🖥️

Clearance: space between objects (e.g., eyes and screen).
Reach: how far you can reach (e.g., to a keyboard).
Workspace envelope: the 3D space you can access while working.

Example: Your workspace envelope is everything you can reach from your chair. 💺

Ergonomics 🪑

Making products comfortable and efficient to use. Includes physical (body fit), cognitive (mental workload), and organizational (work environment) factors.

Example: A computer mouse shaped to fit your hand reduces strain. 🖱️

Physiological & Psychological Factors 💪🧠

Physical (strength, stamina) and mental (attention, memory) factors matter for safety and effectiveness.

Example: Emergency stop buttons are big and red for a reason! 🛑

Inclusive Design ♿

Design for everyone—regardless of age, ability, or background.

Example: Elevators with braille and audio signals help visually impaired users. 🛗

Sample Questions & Model Answers 📝

Q1 (SL/HL): Why do designers use the 5th and 95th percentiles when designing a product?
Model Answer: Designers use the 5th and 95th percentiles to ensure that products are usable by the vast majority of people, not just the average. This helps make products more inclusive and reduces the risk of excluding users at the extremes.
Q2 (SL/HL): Give an example of how psychological factors influence product design.
Model Answer: Designers use color, shape, and layout to make controls easy to find and use. For example, using red for emergency buttons makes them stand out and easy to identify quickly.
Q3 (HL): Explain the importance of biomechanics in ergonomic design.
Model Answer: Biomechanics helps designers understand how forces act on the body during product use. This knowledge allows them to reduce strain, prevent injury, and improve comfort, especially for products used repeatedly or for long periods.

Class Activities 🧑‍🔬

  • Measure the hand span, height, and arm length of classmates. Plot the data and discuss how it would affect the design of a school desk. 📏🧑‍🏫
  • Analyze a common product (e.g., scissors, remote control) for ergonomic strengths and weaknesses. Suggest improvements. ✂️📺
  • Design a poster explaining the difference between static and dynamic data, with examples. 🧍🏃
  • Role-play as different users (child, elderly, left-handed) and test a product for inclusivity. 🎭

Homework 🏠

  • Find a product at home that is uncomfortable or hard to use. Write a paragraph explaining why, using at least three key terms from the vocabulary list. 📝
  • Research a famous ergonomic product (e.g., Aeron chair, OXO Good Grips tools) and explain what makes it successful. 🔍
  • Interview a family member about a product they find easy or hard to use. Summarize your findings and suggest a design improvement. 👨‍👩‍👧‍👦
