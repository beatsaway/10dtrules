Metals: Properties, Ores, and Value Chains ⛏️🔩

From rocks to razor-sharp swords—metals shape our world! ⚡

Key Vocabulary 📚

  • Metal – Hard, shiny, malleable, ductile, good conductor
  • Alloy – Mixture of at least one metal with other metals or non-metals
  • Native metal – Metal found in pure form in nature (e.g., gold, copper)
  • Ore – Rock/mineral from which metal can be profitably extracted
  • Bauxite – Main ore of aluminum
  • Malachite – Copper ore
  • Hematite – Iron ore
  • Smelting – Extracting metal from ore by heating/melting
  • Value chain – Steps that add value from raw material to final product
  • Lattice – Regular arrangement of atoms in a solid
  • Crystalline structure – Repeating pattern of atoms (crystals, grains)
  • Grain – Region of similar crystal structure in a metal
  • Nucleation – Starting point for crystal growth
  • Grain boundary – Edge where grains meet
  • Quenching – Rapid cooling to form small grains (harder, more brittle)
  • Tempering – Heat treatment to increase toughness (differential tempering: katana example)
  • Slip plane – Plane along which atoms in a lattice can slide
  • Dislocation – Defect in lattice that allows deformation

Core Concepts 🧠

What is a Metal?

Metals are hard, shiny, malleable, ductile, and conduct heat/electricity.
Examples: Iron, copper, gold, aluminum.

Ores & Extraction

Most metals are found in ores (e.g., bauxite, malachite, hematite).
Smelting: Heating ore to extract metal.
Fun fact: The Washington Monument's capstone is aluminum!

Value Chains

Value is added as raw materials are processed into metals and products.
Global issue: Raw material countries often get less profit than manufacturing countries.

Lattice & Crystalline Structure

Atoms in metals form regular, repeating patterns (lattices).
Grains: Regions of similar structure.
Nucleation: Where crystals start to grow.
Grain boundaries: Where grains meet—can be weak spots.

Grain Size & Cooling

Rapid cooling (quenching) = small grains (hard, brittle).
Slow cooling = large grains (tough, ductile).
Example: Obsidian (glassy, rapid) vs. granite (grainy, slow).

Tempering

Heat treatment to increase toughness.
Differential tempering: Katana swords have hard, sharp edges (small grains) and flexible spines (large grains).

Slip Planes & Dislocation

Slip planes are planes in the lattice where atoms can slide, allowing metals to deform.
Dislocations: Defects that make deformation easier.

Fun Facts & Real-World 💡

  • The Washington Monument's capstone is aluminum—once more valuable than gold!
  • Obsidian is glassy (rapid cooling), granite is grainy (slow cooling).
  • Katana swords: hard edge, flexible spine—thanks to differential tempering.
