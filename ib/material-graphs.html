Material Graphs & Selection Charts 📊🧩

Graphs make materials make sense—choose wisely! 🔎

Key Vocabulary 📚

  • Material selection chart – Graph showing properties (e.g., strength vs. density) to help pick materials
  • Strength – How much pressure a material can withstand (measured in megapascals, MPa)
  • Density – Mass per unit volume (kg/m³)
  • Cost – How expensive a material is (often on a log scale)
  • Stress – Force per unit area (σ, measured in pascals or MPa)
  • Strain – Change in length/original length (ε, no units)
  • Proportional limit – Point where elastic deformation ends and plastic begins
  • Yield point – Stress beyond which material deforms permanently
  • Ultimate strength – Maximum stress a material can withstand
  • Elastic deformation – Material returns to original shape after stress
  • Plastic deformation – Material is permanently deformed
  • Young's modulus – Slope of the elastic region; measures stiffness

Core Concepts 🧠

Material Selection Charts

Graphs that plot two properties (like strength vs. density or strength vs. cost) to help you pick the right material for your design.
Logarithmic axes: The axes often increase by factors of 10, not linearly!
Example: Foams are light & weak (bottom left), alloys are strong (top), composites are light & strong (top left).

Stress-Strain Graphs

Show how a material responds to force.
Stress (σ): Force per area (y-axis, MPa).
Strain (ε): Change in length/original length (x-axis).
Elastic region: Straight line—material snaps back.
Plastic region: Curved—material is permanently deformed.
Proportional limit: End of elastic, start of plastic.
Yield point: Where permanent deformation begins.
Ultimate strength: Max stress before breaking.

Young's Modulus

The slope of the elastic (straight) region of a stress-strain graph.
High slope = stiff (less strain for same stress).
Low slope = flexible (more strain for same stress).

Fun Facts & Real-World 💡

  • Foams are used for packaging because they're light and don't need to be strong.
  • Alloys are chosen for strength—think airplane parts!
  • Engineers must know the yield point to keep bridges and buildings safe.

Quick Quiz ⚡

What does the slope of the elastic region on a stress-strain graph represent?
Model Answer: Young's modulus—it measures stiffness.
What is the difference between elastic and plastic deformation?
Model Answer: Elastic: material returns to original shape; Plastic: material is permanently deformed.
Why are axes on material selection charts often logarithmic?
Model Answer: To show a wide range of values (e.g., 1 to 10,000) in a compact space.
