Psychological Factors in Design 🧠🎨

Why do we care? Psychological factors shape how we sense, process, and react to the world. Great design means understanding how people think, feel, and respond!

How We Process Information 🔄

Did You Know? 🤔
Your brain is constantly turning sights, sounds, and sensations into actions—often in a split second!

We experience the world through five senses: sight, hearing, touch, taste, and smell. Our bodies act like information processing systems:

  • Input: Light, sound, pressure, heat, taste, or smell
  • Sensory process: Sensory organs (eyes, ears, skin, tongue, nose) detect the input
  • Brain: Processes the information and decides what to do
  • Output: A response (e.g., moving your hand away from something hot)
(Input → Sensory Organs → Brain → Output)
Example: You touch something hot. Your skin senses the heat (input), sends a signal to your brain (processing), and your brain tells your muscles to move your hand (output) — all in a flash!

The Five Senses & Design 👀👂✋👅👃

👀 Sight

  • Visibility: Can users see it clearly? (Think: font size, color contrast, screen glare)
  • Readability: Is the information easy to read?
  • Screen resolution: Does the display make things sharp or blurry?
Real-life: Polarized sunglasses can make some screens unreadable! Designers must consider how products look in different conditions.

👂 Hearing

  • Pitch, frequency, volume: Can users hear alerts or instructions?
  • How sound travels: Sound is compressions and rarefactions of air molecules, not just wavy lines!
  • Amplitude: Higher amplitude = louder sound
Design tip: Use different tones or volumes for different alerts (e.g., louder for emergencies).

✋ Touch

  • Texture: Does it feel good? Does it help grip?
  • Friction: Enough to hold, not so much it's uncomfortable
  • Temperature: Is it comfortable to touch?
  • Skin layers: Epidermis, dermis, hypodermis — each senses differently
Real-life: Coins and your fingerprints have ridges to help you grip. Trackpads need just the right friction!

👅 Taste

  • Survival mechanism: Bitter = warning! (Many toxins are bitter)
  • Design for safety: Some toys have a bitter taste to stop kids eating them
  • Taste buds: Detect salty, bitter, sweet, umami, sour
Design tip: Don't make dangerous things taste sweet!

👃 Smell

  • Emotional triggers: Smells can spark memories and feelings
  • Survival: Bad smells = warning (e.g., spoiled food)
  • Olfactory bulb: Sends signals straight to the brain
Real-life: The smell of something burning or spoiled is a powerful warning!

Design Tips & Real-World Examples 💡

  • Use high-contrast colors and big fonts for visibility
  • Make important sounds louder or unique
  • Add texture for grip where needed
  • Don't make dangerous things taste or smell appealing
  • Use familiar smells, sounds, and visuals to trigger the right emotions
Fun Fact: Some gas companies add a rotten egg smell to gas (which is naturally odorless) so leaks are easy to detect!
