Alloys: Types, Properties, and Superalloys 🧪⚙️

Alloys: stronger, smarter, and everywhere! 🌍

Key Vocabulary 📚

  • Alloy – Mixture of at least one metal with other metals or non-metals
  • Ferrous – Contains iron (Fe), usually magnetic
  • Non-ferrous – Does not contain iron, usually not magnetic
  • Mild steel – Iron + small % carbon; tough, used in engineering
  • High carbon steel – Iron + more carbon; hard, brittle
  • Stainless steel – Iron + nickel + chromium; corrosion-resistant
  • Cast iron – Iron + high % carbon; strong, brittle, cast in molds
  • Aluminum alloy – Aluminum + copper/manganese; lightweight, strong
  • Bronze – Copper + tin; strong, corrosion-resistant
  • Brass – Copper + zinc; decorative, musical instruments
  • Sterling silver – Silver + copper; jewelry, ornaments
  • Superalloy – Alloy with superior strength, heat, and corrosion resistance
  • Malleability – Ability to be shaped or formed
  • Ductility – Ability to be drawn into wire
  • Hardness – Resistance to scratching
  • Patina – Surface layer from oxidation (e.g., green on bronze)

Core Concepts 🧠

What is an Alloy?

An alloy is a mixture of at least one metal with other metals or non-metals.
Why alloys? They are usually stronger, harder, and less malleable than pure metals because foreign atoms disrupt the metal lattice.

Ferrous vs Non-Ferrous Alloys

Ferrous: Contains iron, usually magnetic (e.g., steel, cast iron).
Non-ferrous: No iron, usually not magnetic (e.g., aluminum alloys, bronze, brass).

Common Ferrous Alloys

  • Mild steel: Tough, used in engineering.
  • High carbon steel: Hard, brittle.
  • Stainless steel: Corrosion-resistant, cookware.
  • Cast iron: Strong, brittle, engine blocks, cookware.

Common Non-Ferrous Alloys

  • Aluminum alloys: Lightweight, strong, airplanes, window frames.
  • Bronze: Copper + tin, ships, statues, corrosion-resistant.
  • Brass: Copper + zinc, decorative, musical instruments, doorknobs.
  • Sterling silver: Silver + copper, jewelry, ornaments.

Superalloys

Alloys with superior strength, heat, and corrosion resistance.
Used in: Jet engines, turbines, high-stress parts.

Patina & Corrosion

Patina is a surface layer from oxidation (e.g., green on bronze).
Fun fact: The Golden Gate Bridge is always being painted to prevent corrosion!

Fun Facts & Real-World 💡

  • Bronze Age: bronze tools changed history!
  • Ships use bronze to avoid rust; bridges are painted to prevent corrosion.
  • Superalloys keep jet engines running at extreme temperatures.
