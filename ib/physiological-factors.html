Physiological Factors in Design 💪🦾

Why do they matter? Physiological factors are about how our bodies work—comfort, fatigue, strength, and movement. Great design means making things usable for everyone, at every age and ability!

What Are Physiological Factors? 🦾

  • Comfort: Feeling physically or psychologically at ease
  • Fatigue: Physical or mental tiredness
  • Biomechanics: How our bodies move (joints, muscles, posture, force)
  • Muscle Strength: How much force we can use (changes with age!)
  • Mechanical Advantage: Using tools or machines to make tasks easier
  • Torque: A force that causes rotation (think: opening a jar)

Comfort & Fatigue 🛋️😴

Designing for Comfort

Comfort is about making users feel at ease. Sometimes, we want people to relax—like in airport lounges designed for long layovers.

Example: Singapore Changi Airport lounges are designed for napping and comfort during long waits.

Designing for Discomfort

Sometimes, we want to limit comfort—like benches with barriers to prevent sleeping. This encourages short stays and keeps spaces available for more people.

Example: Anti-sleep benches in train stations and airports are designed to discourage people from lying down for long periods.

Why Fatigue Matters

Fatigue (tiredness) can cause user errors, slow reaction times, and reduce safety. Good design can help reduce fatigue and keep users alert and safe.

Biomechanics in Design 🏃‍♂️🦵

What is Biomechanics?

Biomechanics is the study of how our bodies move—joints, muscles, tendons, posture, force, repetition, and duration. Designers use biomechanics to make products safer and more comfortable.

Gathering Biomechanical Data

Designers use motion capture and other tools to study how people move. This helps them design products that fit real users.

Example: Motion capture suits track joint movement for ergonomic analysis.

Real-World Example: Helmets

Helmet design has evolved to reduce fatigue and increase comfort and safety:

  • WWI: Heavy steel helmets protected from shrapnel but were exhausting to wear.
  • WWII: Redesigned for more comfort and better protection.
  • Modern: Lightweight, breathable, and much safer—can be worn longer without fatigue.

Real-World Example: Backpacks

Old backpacks put all the weight on your shoulders, causing pain and fatigue. Modern backpacks use hip straps and breathable mesh to distribute weight and reduce sweat, making them much more comfortable for long use.

Muscle Strength & Mechanical Advantage 💪⚙️

Muscle Strength Changes with Age

People are strongest in young adulthood. As we age, muscle strength decreases, so products need to be designed for all users—not just the strongest!

Mechanical Advantage & Torque

Tools like levers, jar openers, and long door handles use mechanical advantage and torque to make tasks easier for everyone.

Example: Jar openers and long door handles make it easier for people with less strength to open things.

Design Tips & Real-World Examples 💡

  • Design for comfort when users need to relax or focus for long periods
  • Use discomfort to encourage short stays (e.g., anti-sleep benches)
  • Distribute weight in backpacks and seats to reduce fatigue
  • Use mechanical advantage (levers, handles) to help all users
  • Test products with users of different ages and abilities
Fun Fact: The best design reduces fatigue, increases comfort, and keeps everyone safe!
